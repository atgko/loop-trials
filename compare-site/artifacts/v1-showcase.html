Loops Explaining Loops

A 10-minute page about agentic loops — written, in part, by one.

What is an agentic loop?

Normally, when you use an AI chatbot, it's one question, one answer. You ask, it replies, done. An agentic loop is different: instead of answering once, the AI repeats a cycle of reasoning and acting — over and over — until some goal is met or it runs out of budget.

This is different from a chatbot that just remembers earlier messages in a conversation. A long chat history is still one exchange at a time, with a person deciding what happens next after every reply. In an agentic loop, the AI decides what happens next itself, for as many steps as it takes — a person doesn't have to sit there clicking "continue." The AI keeps going on its own until it either succeeds or hits its stopping condition.

The cycle usually looks like this: the AI thinks about what to do next, takes an action (like running code, searching the web, or editing a file), looks at the result, and decides whether it's done or needs another pass. This pattern has a name in AI research — ReAct (short for "Reason + Act") — introduced in a widely-cited 2022 research paper, and it's the basic skeleton underneath most AI agents built since.

Repeat or Stop

The loop needs a stopping condition — a rule for when to quit. Common ones: the goal is achieved (tests pass, the task is done), a maximum number of steps is hit, or a human interrupts it. Without a stopping condition, a loop can run forever, which is exactly the kind of thing that goes wrong in practice (more on that later).

You've likely already seen this pattern without the label. AI coding assistants that read a codebase, make an edit, run the tests, and try again on their own are running an agentic loop. So are research assistants that decide what to search for, look at the results, and decide whether they need to search again before answering. In both cases, nobody is manually approving every intermediate step — the loop is running itself against a goal.

When should you use one?

Not every task needs a loop. If a task is one step — summarize this paragraph, translate this sentence — a single AI call is faster, cheaper, and just as good. Loops earn their cost when a task is multi-step and the right next step depends on what happened in the previous one.

A good rule of thumb: use an agentic loop when the task has a way to check its own progress — a test suite that passes or fails, a compiler that errors or doesn't, a checklist that's either complete or not. That gives the loop something concrete to react to at each step. Tasks with no clear "am I done yet?" signal are much harder to hand to a loop, because it has no way to know when to stop trying — which is a large part of why this very experiment (see below) had to define an explicit scoring rule up front, instead of just letting the AI decide when the page "felt" finished.

Here's a worked comparison. Say you want an AI to fix a bug in a small program.

  • Single call: you paste the code and the error message, the AI guesses a fix in one shot, and you paste it back in yourself to see if it worked. If it didn't, you're back at the start, manually.
  • Agentic loop: the AI edits the file itself, runs the program, reads the new error (if any), and tries again — automatically, without you in the middle — until the program runs clean or it hits a step limit.

The second version costs more calls and more time. It's worth it here because "did the program run without errors?" is a check the loop can perform on its own, over and over, without asking you. A quick way to decide which situation you're in:

  • Does the task need more than one step to finish? If no, skip the loop.
  • Can something (a test, a compiler, a checklist) tell you objectively whether a given attempt succeeded? If no, a loop won't know when to stop either.
  • Can you tolerate the extra time and cost of several AI calls instead of one? If not, a loop isn't worth it even if the first two answers are yes.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Handles multi-step work. A loop can plan, act, check, and correct itself across many steps without a human re-prompting it each time.
  • Self-correction. If step 3 reveals that step 1 was wrong, the loop can notice and fix it on step 4, instead of a human catching it later.
  • Takes real actions. It can call tools that actually change something — run code, query a database, send a file — not just produce text.

Cons

  • Slower and more expensive. A single AI reply might take a second. A loop chains many replies together, so a task can take minutes and cost far more.
  • Can spiral. Researchers have documented "hallucination spirals," where a coding agent's small early mistake compounds over several loop iterations into a much bigger, self-reinforcing failure.
  • Hard to grade its own work. If the AI itself is also checking whether it's done, it can be too easy on itself — a documented pattern researchers call self-attribution bias. That's a real risk for exactly this kind of project.
Diminishing (or negative) returns: studies of AI agents re-checking and re-fixing their own work have found that most of the improvement shows up after the first extra pass — later passes help less, and for some models an extra round can actually make things worse, by widening what gets changed and introducing new problems along the way. More loop iterations is not automatically better.

The example is this page

This lecture page is itself the output of an agentic loop. An AI played two roles across several rounds: a Builder, who wrote or revised the page, and a Critic, who scored the current draft and pointed out specific problems. Each round, the Builder responded to the Critic's actual feedback, and the loop kept going until the draft hit a strict score on both content and design, or a fixed round limit was reached — whichever came first.

There's a catch, though, and it's the same one named in the Cons section above: if the same reasoning that wrote a draft is also the reasoning that scores it, the score can drift upward for reasons that have nothing to do with quality — familiarity, fatigue, or just wanting to be done. This page's own process tried to guard against that directly: before the Critic role is allowed to award a 9 or a 10 on either axis, it has to point to the exact sentence, element, or line of code that earns each point — not just assert a number. A score with no quotable evidence behind it doesn't count.

That's the "am I done yet?" signal from the "When should you use one?" section, made concrete: a numeric rubric instead of a vague feeling of "looks good." If you want to see the whole process — every draft, every critique, and how the scores actually moved round to round — that's in the companion file that came with this page.

Recap

An agentic loop is a repeating reason-act-observe cycle an AI runs on its own until a stopping condition is met — not a single reply, and not just a long conversation with a human approving every step. Use one when a task takes more than one step and has some objective way to check whether a given attempt worked; skip it when a single response would do just as well.

The trade-off is real in both directions. The upside is genuine autonomy: a loop can plan, act, notice its own mistakes, and correct course across many steps without anyone hovering over it. The downside is cost, latency, and the risk that the loop either spirals into a bigger mistake than the one it started with, or grades its own homework a little too kindly along the way.

If you take one thing from this page, take the checklist from "When should you use one?" — it's the fastest way to tell whether a problem in front of you actually needs a loop, or just needs one good AI response.
