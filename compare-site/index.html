Loops Explaining Loops
v1 vs v2

Two runs of the same experiment: have an AI loop build and grade its own 10-minute lecture page on agentic loops, with no human in the loop between rounds. v1 used a subjective 0–10 design score graded by the same session that wrote the draft. v2 changed the mechanism — a mechanical scoring formula, an isolated cold critic, and real browser rendering compared against reference images — to see whether that closes the gap v1 exposed.

What changed between runs

  • Mechanical design score. v2 derives the Design score from a fixed checklist and formula in design-system.md, not a subjective 0–10 impression.
  • Isolated critic. v2's critic runs as a separate subagent that never sees the builder's reasoning, prior scores, or the round number — it grades every round cold.
  • Real rendering. v2's critic has live browser access: it screenshots the actual rendered page and compares it against five reference images extracted from a real slide deck, not just the written rules.
  • Two deliverables. v2 produces both a clean standalone page meant to be shared (showcase.html) and the full round-by-round process view, instead of just the latter.

The part worth reading, from each run

v1 finding

Every 9 or 10 in every round pointed at something real — a quoted line, a word count, a measured contrast ratio. Nothing was rubber-stamped. And yet, flip from the round 1 tab to the round 4 tab in final.html and the layout, type, color palette, and overall feel are almost identical. What changed round to round was accuracy, completeness, and one contrast fix — not the actual visual design. A rigorous, evidence-backed loop grading itself can converge on “passes every check I wrote for it” without ever being asked the fuzzier question that actually mattered. The rubric never asked.

v2 finding

Design scored 9/10 for seven rounds straight — not the same bug recurring, but seven genuinely different, real defects, each caught by the rendered check and each backed by concrete evidence. Read rounds 2, 6, and 8 together, though, and one thread runs through all three: the reference images fill every icon badge solid crimson, while the design system's own dominance rule caps how much of the page can be crimson-filled. Those two constraints are in real tension. Because each critic call is deliberately isolated and cold, no single round was positioned to name that conflict — it only shows up when you read all eight reports side by side. The loop hit its cap before anyone named it explicitly.

Put side by side, the two runs show two different failure modes of the same pattern: v1's subjective, self-graded rubric converged to a perfect score without the thing it was meant to measure ever changing. v2's objective, cold-isolated rubric caught real bugs every single round — and still plateaued, because isolation that prevents rubber-stamping also prevents any round from noticing a pattern that only exists across rounds.
