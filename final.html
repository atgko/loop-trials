How this page came together

The lecture page you just read (or skimmed) wasn't written in one pass. It was produced by an AI running the same kind of process the page itself describes: a loop. This tab is the log of that loop, written for you rather than as an internal record.

The process, briefly

Each round, the same AI took on two roles. First, as "the Builder," it wrote or revised the page. Then, as "the Critic," it reviewed that draft as if seeing it for the first time, scored it on two things — Content (is it accurate, complete, and does it actually teach the topic in about 10 minutes?) and Design (is it easy to follow, readable, and polished?) — and wrote down specific problems. The next round, the Builder had to fix exactly those problems, not just make a generally "nicer" page. The loop would stop as soon as both scores hit a perfect 10 in the same round, or after 8 rounds, whichever came first.

What changed, round by round

Round 1 — Content 8/10, Design 7/10
This was the first draft: it covered what an agentic loop is, when to use one, and its pros and cons, but it read in about 5 minutes instead of the promised 10, overstated who published a key research paper, and had a couple of small layout bugs (a diagram that could visually break on a narrow window, and a styled box that was defined but never used).
Round 1 (Content 8/10, Design 7/10) → Round 2 (Content 9/10, Design 8/10)
The "when should you use one?" section was too thin, so it grew a worked example (fixing a bug with one AI reply vs. with a loop) and a short decision checklist; the overstated research claim was reworded more carefully; and a live "Section X of Y · ~N min left" indicator was added so a reader always knows how far through the page they are.
Round 2 (Content 9/10, Design 8/10) → Round 3 (Content 10/10, Design 9/10)
That new "min left" indicator did its job a little too well — it revealed that the page still only took about 6 minutes to read, undercutting its own "10-minute page" claim. Two sections that had stayed thin ("This page is the example" and the closing recap) were substantially expanded with real content, bringing the reading time to just under 10 minutes. A smaller design fix also landed: three different boxed elements on the page (a comparison layout, a callout, and a checklist) had each picked a different, unrelated border style, so they were unified into one consistent pattern.
Round 3 (Content 10/10, Design 9/10) → Round 4 (Content 10/10, Design 10/10)
Content held steady at a perfect score. The one remaining design problem was a color: an orange used for a highlighted note and a small arrow icon looked fine at a glance, but when actually measured against readability guidelines, it didn't have enough contrast against its background for reliable reading. It was swapped for a darker shade of the same color — orange stayed orange, but now it's legible — which closed the gap and brought both scores to a perfect 10 at the same time.

How it ended

Stop reason: perfect score. The loop was allowed to run up to 8 rounds no matter what. It didn't need all 8 — both scores reached a genuine 10/10 in the same round (Round 4), so the loop stopped there, 4 rounds in. This page is not claiming a perfect score it didn't earn: each score above was checked point-by-point against a written rubric before being finalized, specifically to guard against the AI grading its own work too kindly — the same "self-attribution bias" problem the lecture page itself warns about in its Cons section.
